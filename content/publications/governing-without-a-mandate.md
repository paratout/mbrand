The target architecture was good. I still think so. It was coherent, it was cheaper than what existed, and it solved problems every unit had complained about. I presented it to the group that had to approve it, and it did not get rejected - something worse happened. It got thanked. Politely, warmly, at length, and then nothing moved for four months.

What I had missed was structural, not presentational. In that organization nobody could be told to do anything. The units were legally and financially their own entities. The central body held the name, the shared platforms, and a convening role, but not a reporting line. The decision body I had presented to was deliberately balanced so that no single interest could carry a vote alone. Adoption was voluntary in the strict sense: any unit could nod, walk out, and continue exactly as before, forever, with no consequence.

*In a federation, your design is not a plan. It is an offer.*

This piece is the long version of two things I have written before in passing - that you have to [go there in person](/publications/landscape-due-diligence), and that change is people first - because in this kind of organization those two lines stop being good practice and become the entire operating model. Composited from my own missions across federated and member-based structures, blurred as always, and with the shape of the organization generalized on purpose: it fits alliances, cooperatives, franchise networks, professional bodies, groups of national member organizations, and any holding whose units bought their autonomy fairly and intend to keep it.

## The shape of the place

Before the method, the terrain, because every technique below is a response to a specific feature of it.

**Autonomy is real, not rhetorical.** Units have their own legal identity, their own funding, their own staff, and often their own board. "Central" is a service and a convener. When a unit says no, that is the end of the sentence, not the start of an escalation.

**Governance is balanced by design.** The bodies that decide are composed so that no single interest group can dominate: seats are allocated across constituencies, and the rules are written to protect the smaller and the poorer from being outvoted by the larger and the richer. This is not dysfunction - it is the constitutional promise that keeps the federation together, and any change proposal that quietly erodes it will be resisted by people who like the proposal.

**Resources are wildly asymmetric.** One unit has an IT team of thirty; another has a person who does IT alongside three other jobs. A platform that is trivial for the first is existential for the second. Designing for the average unit means designing for nobody.

**Local constraints are hard and legitimate.** Data residency, labour law, funding restrictions, language, an existing local system that a regulator approved, a partner contract signed for six years. In a corporate group these get overruled. Here they are simply the shape of the world.

**The mission is the currency.** People are usually there for the purpose rather than the pay, which cuts both ways: enormous goodwill toward anything that visibly serves the mission, and enormous resistance to anything that smells like central control dressed as efficiency.

![Two governance worlds - what changes in every part of the architect's job when the mandate is gone](/images/pub/governing-without-a-mandate/two-worlds-v1.png)

The single most useful thing I can tell anyone arriving from a corporate environment: **almost every instinct you have is inverted here**. In a corporate group you design, then socialize. Here you socialize, then design, and the socializing *is* the design work rather than the packaging around it. In a corporate group a standard is enforced by a gate. Here it is enforced by whether the third unit finds it easier than their own way. In a corporate group the risk is that people comply without believing. Here the risk is that they believe and still cannot act, because the money or the law says no.

## People first, and not for the reason you think

"Change is people first" is usually said as a humane sentiment. In a federation it is a mechanical statement. There is no other enforcement layer. The people *are* the enforcement layer, and specifically these people: the one person in each unit who will either try your thing or not.

Which means the unit of work is not the architecture. It is the relationship that will decide whether the architecture is ever tried, and that relationship has properties you can actually manage: it has to be direct, it has to be reciprocal, and it has to survive a no.

**Direct** means you know them and they know you. Not the unit - the person. In a federation of twenty units there are perhaps forty people who matter for any given change, and you can know forty people.

**Reciprocal** means they get something before you ask for something. This is the part most central functions skip. Your first interaction with a unit should not be a request. It should be a small piece of help that costs you an afternoon and saves them a week: a fix, a review, a template, an introduction to another unit that already solved their problem.

**Surviving a no** means the relationship does not change when they decline. If a unit says no to your pilot and your next three emails are colder, everyone in the network knows within a month, and your consent-based governance turns into a room full of people managing you rather than working with you. Federations are small worlds. Reputation travels faster than any communication plan.

> Central functions that treat units as an audience get compliance theatre. Central functions that treat units as customers get adoption. Central functions that treat units as *members* get advocates, which is the only thing that scales.

## Go there in person, properly

Now the core practice, and the reason this piece exists. In a corporate landscape, visiting the markets is good practice - it improves your inventory and builds goodwill. In a federation it is the mechanism itself, and doing it remotely does not degrade the result by twenty percent; it produces a different and much weaker artifact.

Three reasons, all of which I learned by getting them wrong first.

**What you need is not in the answers.** You are collecting constraints, informal power, and unspoken history. On a call people give you the official version, because the official version is safe and calls are short. In a room, over two days, the official version cracks around the second coffee, when someone says "well, in practice we do it differently because of what happened three years ago" - and that sentence is the one you flew for.

**Presence is the reciprocity.** In an organization where you have nothing to offer but attention, showing up in person is the offer. It costs you time and budget, visibly, on their behalf. A unit that has never had a central colleague sit in their office knows exactly what that means, and so does a unit that has.

**The room is a demonstration.** How you behave in their space - whether you listen, whether you bring your own agenda or theirs, whether you take notes - is the evidence they will use to decide whether the governance you are proposing will feel like this or like something else. You are not describing a governance culture. You are performing it.

![The visit - what to do before, the two-day shape on site, what you write back within the week, and the things you must never bring](/images/pub/governing-without-a-mandate/visit-v1.png)

### Before: earn the two days

Send one page, not a deck. What you want to understand, what you will do with it, what they will get back, and - the sentence that changes the temperature of the whole visit - what will *not* happen as a result of this conversation. In every federation I have worked in, the unspoken question behind a central visit is whether this is the beginning of losing something. Answer it before you arrive, in writing, and then honour it exactly.

Ask them to set the agenda for half the time. Ask who else should be in the room and take their answer, including the person they warn you is difficult - that person is usually the one who has been burned by a previous central initiative, and they are the most valuable interview of the trip.

Never send a target architecture in advance. If your picture arrives before you do, the visit becomes a negotiation about your picture instead of an inquiry into their world.

### During: two days, mostly listening

Day one is theirs. Walk the work: sit with the people who use the systems, in the place they use them, doing the actual task. Not a demo of the system - the task. You will see the three spreadsheets that sit beside the official tool, and nobody will have hidden them, because in their world those spreadsheets are not shameful, they are how the job gets done.

Day two is shared: the constraint conversation, the capability map, and the first honest exchange about what central could plausibly do that would help. Somewhere in day two you will feel the temptation to start pitching. Do not. The pitch has a much higher hit rate in month three, in writing, after they have seen you deliver something small.

Four questions carry most of the value, and they are deliberately not about systems:

- How does this work get done here today, and who actually does it?
- What would break for you if this changed, and who would you have to ask?
- What have you built or bought locally that you would recommend to another unit?
- What has central done before that made your life harder, and what would you want us to promise not to repeat?

That last question is uncomfortable to ask and it is the one that buys you the most. It gets you the history, the grudge, and the specific behaviour you must avoid, in one answer. Write down the answer verbatim and repeat it back to them in your write-up, unedited.

### After: the write-back, within the week

The visit produces exactly one obligation, and missing it costs more than not visiting at all: a written summary back to them, within a week, containing what you heard, what you understood as constraints, what you noticed that they might want to share with other units, and what you will do next - including "nothing, for now" where that is the honest answer.

Send it to them first, before anyone central sees it, and say so. The one line that has done more for my credibility in federated settings than anything else: *"This goes to you first. Tell me what I got wrong or what you would rather I did not repeat, and I will change it before it goes anywhere else."*

### The realistic budget

A federation of twenty units cannot be visited exhaustively in a first year, and pretending otherwise leads to a schedule that collapses in month four. What has worked for me: visit six to eight units in the first year, chosen deliberately - two large and influential, two small and constrained, two that are sceptical, one or two that are already innovating - and reach the rest by video with a shortened protocol. Then repeat the physical visits on a rolling basis so that every unit has been seen within about two years and no unit feels like a permanent second tier.

Video visits work only if they inherit the discipline: the one-pager before, the half-agenda handed over, the write-back within a week, and the camera pointed at the work rather than at slides. What they cannot replicate is the corridor.

## Reading and recording constraints

The output of all that travel is not a report. It is a **constraint register**, and it is the most valuable document a federated architecture function owns, because it is the thing that lets you design something that can actually be said yes to.

![Six kinds of local constraint - how each one shows up, what you can do about it, and the test that separates a constraint from a preference](/images/pub/governing-without-a-mandate/constraints-v1.png)

Six kinds, and the response differs completely by kind.

**Legal and regulatory.** Data residency, retention, employment law, sector rules. Non-negotiable, and the design must simply absorb them - usually as a deployment or data-location option rather than a different system. Record the actual citation, not the rumour: a surprising share of "we are not allowed to" turns out, when someone finally reads the rule, to be "we were told once, in 2019, and nobody checked since".

**Funding.** The unit has no capital budget, or its funding is restricted to specific purposes and cannot pay for shared infrastructure. This is the constraint most often mistaken for reluctance. A unit that says "we are not ready" often means "we cannot spend money on this in this budget cycle", which is a completely different problem with completely different solutions - central hosting, staged fees, a slot in next year's cycle.

**Capacity.** No people, or one person with four jobs. Anything you design that requires a local administrator will fail here regardless of enthusiasm. This constraint sets your ceiling on complexity for the whole federation, and it is the one large units most consistently forget when they design on your behalf.

**Language and culture.** Working language, documentation language, the local norms about disagreement in meetings. If your artifacts exist only in one language, you have quietly restricted your governance to those fluent in it, and they will be the ones from the wealthier units. Translation is not politeness here, it is enfranchisement.

**Political and mandate.** The unit's own board or membership has a say. Their director cannot commit without a decision you are not in the room for. Your proposals need to be shaped so they can survive *that* room too, which means giving your counterpart the material they need to argue your case when you are not there - a two-pager they can forward, not a deck they must present.

**Technical and legacy.** The regulator-approved system, the six-year contract, the platform their largest partner mandates. Usually the most tractable of the six, and almost always the one central spends the most time on, because it is the one that feels like architecture.

**The judgment that matters most** is separating constraint from preference, and the honest answer is that you often cannot, at first, and should not try too hard. The test I use: a constraint has a *source outside the person telling me about it* - a law, a contract, a budget line, a board decision - and can therefore be named, dated, and eventually revisited. A preference is a choice defended as a constraint, and it is usually protecting something reasonable: a team that just finished a painful migration, a local system someone built and is proud of, a fear of losing control. Never call a preference a preference out loud. Record it in the register as "stated as a constraint, source not yet identified", keep working, and let time and peer examples resolve it. Half of them dissolve on their own within a year, quietly, and the people who let them go should be allowed to do so without anyone noticing.

## Designing a core thin enough to be accepted

Now the architecture, which in this context is mostly a question of *scope discipline*. Everything you centralize costs you political capital that you will need later, so the design problem is not "what could be shared" but "what must be".

Ostrom's work on long-lived self-governing institutions has a principle that maps onto this almost directly: in large systems, rules should be organized as multiple nested layers, with local governance at the base and higher layers handling only what the local level cannot ([Ostrom's eight design principles](https://wiki.p2pfoundation.net/Elinor_Ostrom%E2%80%99s_Eight_Commons_Governance_Design_Principles)). That is subsidiarity, and it is a better architectural rule for a federation than any technology principle you will write.

![The thin core - what genuinely has to be shared, the profile layer that absorbs local variation, the local freedom zone, and the subsidiarity test that keeps the boundary honest](/images/pub/governing-without-a-mandate/thin-core-v1.png)

The **subsidiarity test**, applied to every candidate for the core: *can this be decided or run at unit level without breaking something shared?* If yes, it stays local, even when central could do it better, cheaper, and faster. Especially then, in fact, because "we could do it better" is the argument that has historically preceded every central overreach the federation still remembers.

What survives that test is usually a short list: **identity**, because people move between units and partners need one front door; **shared definitions** - the handful of terms and data structures that must mean the same thing everywhere for any group-level number to exist; **the register of record** for whatever the federation exists to hold in common; **the exchange contract**, meaning the interfaces and formats by which units and central talk, rather than the systems on either end; and **the shared platforms that only make sense once**, typically the expensive ones that no single unit could justify alone.

Everything else - the local tools, the workflow, the reporting, the sequence of steps, the vendor choices - stays where it is. Writing that list down explicitly, including the "we will not centralize these" half, is one of the highest-trust artifacts you can produce.

Between the thin core and the local freedom zone sits the piece that makes the whole thing work: **the profile layer**. Rather than one implementation, the shared standard is expressed at levels of conformance - a minimum that everyone can genuinely reach, and one or two richer levels for units that can go further. A small unit sending a monthly file that conforms to the minimum profile is *complying*, not lagging. A large unit running the full interface is not virtuous, it is just bigger. Making partial adoption a first-class, named state rather than a failure is the difference between eighty percent participation and thirty.

Two more design habits that pay for themselves in these settings. **Reversibility**: every shared thing should have a documented way out, and you should say so unprompted, because a unit that knows it can leave finds it much easier to join. And **no forced migration**: new arrivals adopt the current version, existing implementations move when they are touched anyway - the same rule that keeps [pattern revisions](/publications/architecture-patterns) from becoming a tax.

## Building the coalition

With a thin core designed and a constraint register in hand, the temptation is to take the whole thing to the governing body and ask for endorsement. That sequence is backwards, and I have run it backwards more than once.

Endorsement before evidence produces the polite thank-you I described at the top. Evidence before endorsement produces a decision that units have already effectively made. So: pilot first, with volunteers, and bring the governing body a proposal that is already partly true.

![The adoption ladder - the five rungs a unit climbs, what each rung needs from you, what it produces, and the two ways central usually falls off it](/images/pub/governing-without-a-mandate/ladder-v1.png)

**Choose the first two or three carefully.** Not the largest unit, whose scale distorts the design and whose participation reads to everyone else as the centre's favourite. Not the loudest sceptic, whose conversion you are fantasising about. Choose units with a real problem your thing solves, enough capacity to survive a pilot, and a leader whose peers listen to them. One of the three should be small and constrained, because a pilot that only works in well-resourced units has proved nothing about the federation.

**Publish what the pilot actually cost.** Effort, delays, the things that broke, the local workaround that turned out to be necessary. A pilot report that reads like a success story converts nobody, because everyone in a federation has watched a central success story that did not survive contact with their own unit. A pilot report with a section titled "what was harder than we said it would be" gets forwarded.

**Let the peer do the talking.** The single highest-leverage moment in the whole cycle is a fifteen-minute slot where the pilot unit - not you - tells the others what happened, and takes their questions while you sit quietly. It converts at a rate no central presentation ever matches, because the audience trusts the speaker's incentives. Your job is to arrange the room, prepare nothing on their behalf, and resist the urge to correct anything short of a factual error.

**Then take it to the body**, with three or four units already on the ladder, the cost published, and a proposal that asks for something modest: recognition of the profile as the shared way, funding for the shared parts, and permission to keep going. Not a mandate - never a mandate. Even if you could get one voted, a mandate in a federation converts a growing coalition into a compliance exercise, and the units that were about to volunteer become units that are being told, which changes their behaviour and their pace.

The ladder itself is worth naming explicitly, because it changes how you spend your week: **aware** (they know it exists and what it is for), **interested** (they have asked a real question), **piloting** (something is running, however small), **adopting** (it is their normal way), **advocating** (they are explaining it to other units without you). Each rung needs a different thing from you - information, a conversation, hands-on help, patience, and finally a platform. Tracking which unit is on which rung, and what the single next step is for each, is more useful than any programme plan I have ever drawn in this kind of organization.

## Writing a proposal that survives a consent-based room

Balanced-interest bodies mostly do not vote things down. They defer them. A deferral looks gentle and costs a quarter, and the way to avoid it is to write the proposal so that the objections it will attract are already integrated before the meeting.

Consent-based decision practice - the discipline of asking not "do you agree" but "do you have a reasoned objection", and treating objections as information to integrate rather than obstacles ([sociocracy's consent decision making](https://www.sociocracyforall.org/consent-decision-making/)) - describes what most of these bodies do informally. Making it explicit helps enormously, because it moves the bar from *enthusiasm*, which you will never get from everyone, to *good enough for now and safe enough to try*, which you often can.

![Anatomy of a proposal built for consent - the seven parts, the objection round, and the three clauses that unlock a hesitant room](/images/pub/governing-without-a-mandate/proposal-v1.png)

The parts that matter most, in my experience of these rooms:

**Name what each constituency gains and gives up.** Not one benefits section - one paragraph per interest group represented in the room, written honestly enough that a member of that group would recognise themselves. If a group gives up nothing, say that too. The proposal that only describes benefits reads as marketing and gets deferred by people who cannot yet articulate why they are uneasy.

**Offer options, not a single answer.** Two or three, with the trade-offs, and your recommendation stated. A room that is handed one option can only accept or defer; a room that is handed three is doing design, and a room doing design has ownership.

**Include the three unlocking clauses.** A **sunset**: this decision expires in eighteen months unless renewed, so the room is not deciding forever. A **review trigger**: named conditions under which we come back early. An **opt-out or profile**: the explicit statement of what a unit that cannot participate should do instead, so that voting yes does not feel like voting to constrain a peer. I have watched all three of these turn a deferral into a decision in the room, and the sunset clause in particular costs almost nothing: proposals that work get renewed without discussion.

**Pre-wire, openly.** Talk to each constituency before the meeting, show them the draft, and ask what would make them object. Then tell the whole room that you did this, and with whom. Quiet pre-wiring is lobbying and it will eventually be discovered and resented; announced pre-wiring is preparation, and it turns the meeting into a confirmation of work already done rather than a first encounter with a surprise.

**Run the objection round properly.** Clarifying questions first, and hold the line on "clarifying" - the first three questions in any such room are usually opinions wearing a question mark. Then quick reactions. Then, explicitly and one at a time: "do you have an objection, and what is the specific harm you foresee?" An objection must name a harm to the shared thing, not a preference for a different option. Then integrate: modify the proposal in the room where you can, park what needs work with a named person and a date, and take the amended version.

**Write the record while everyone is present**, read it aloud, and publish it the same day in the languages the federation actually works in. In a federated body, the decision record is doing something extra: it is the artifact each representative takes back to a room you will never attend, which is where the real decision to participate gets made.

## Measuring a thing nobody has to do

Compliance rate is unavailable and, worse, misleading - it will be near zero for a year and then jump, and neither number tells you whether you are winning.

What I track instead, in a simple sheet, updated monthly:

**Ladder distribution.** How many units on each rung, and movement since last month. This is the health metric. A federation where nobody moved a rung in a quarter has a stalled change, whatever the activity level looks like.

**Time to first pilot** from first contact. Shortening over the year means your offer is getting clearer and your reputation is doing some of the work.

**Unit-to-unit referrals.** How many conversations happened between units without central in the room. This is the number that predicts everything else, and the day it exceeds your own outreach is the day the thing has become self-sustaining.

**Objection themes.** Categorise what people object to, month by month. Objections migrating from "we do not trust central" to "the profile does not fit our data model" is enormous progress, and no other metric will show it.

**Constraint resolution.** How many recorded constraints turned out to be soft, were removed, or got a workaround. This is the register earning its keep.

**Participation.** Attendance at the forums, and specifically attendance by the smaller units, which is your early-warning system for a governance drifting toward the well-resourced.

The arc, in my experience: months one to six look like nothing is happening while you travel and write; months six to twelve produce two or three pilots and a lot of quiet interest; somewhere between twelve and twenty-four months a threshold passes - roughly a third of units on the ladder past piloting - and the dynamic inverts, with new units asking to join rather than being asked. That inversion is the whole objective, and it is invisible until about a month before it happens.

## Where this goes wrong

**The mandate reflex.** Under pressure for results, someone senior proposes making it mandatory. It will be presented as decisiveness. In a federation it converts volunteers into subjects, stops the referral engine, and hands every sceptic the argument they have been waiting for. If you lose that fight, at minimum insist that the mandate applies only to the thin core and arrives with funding.

**Designing for the reference unit.** The big well-staffed unit is the easiest to work with and the most dangerous to design for. Every requirement gathered there needs a "and what does this mean for a unit of four people" test before it enters the design.

**Extractive engagement.** Visiting to collect data and never returning anything. Two rounds of this and the doors stay open but the answers go official. The write-back within a week is the antidote, and it must contain something they can use.

**The English-only artifact set.** Governance in a language half the federation reads with effort is governance by the fluent. Translate the decisive documents, budget for it, and notice who stops speaking in meetings when you do not.

**Pilot theatre.** A pilot designed to succeed, in a unit chosen because it would succeed, reported as though it proves something. Everyone can smell it, and it costs more credibility than a failed pilot honestly reported.

**Confusing the room with the decision.** The body approved it; nothing happened. In a federation the vote is permission, not adoption. The work after the decision is the same relationship work as before it, and budgeting attention as though approval were the finish line is the most common way these programmes quietly end.

## The kit

Three files in the [library](/library), free to use and adapt.

The **field kit** (document) holds the pre-visit note that says what will not happen, the two-day agenda with the half handed to the host, the interview guide by role including the four questions above, the constraint capture sheet, the write-back template, and the remote adaptation for the units you cannot reach this year.

The **adoption tracker** (spreadsheet) holds the unit register with contacts, autonomy and capacity notes; the constraint register with type, source, status and review date; the ladder tracker with a rung and one next step per unit; a consent decision log with objections and how each was integrated; and a dashboard computing ladder distribution, referral counts, and constraint resolution.

The **proposal pack** (deck) is the template for taking something to a balanced-interest body: the one-page proposal, the per-constituency gains and gives, options with reversibility, the three unlocking clauses, the objection round script, and the decision record format to publish the same day.

## What it teaches you

I came to this kind of organization thinking it would be a slower version of corporate architecture, with more meetings. It is not slower - it is differently shaped. Nothing moves for a long time and then a lot moves at once, and the part that determines whether the second thing ever happens is almost entirely the quality of a few dozen relationships and the honesty of a register of constraints.

The transferable lesson, the one I have taken back into organizations that do have mandates: the mandate was never doing as much work as I thought. Even in a corporate group, the systems that get used are the ones somebody wanted, and the standards that hold are the ones that made someone's week easier. A federation just removes the illusion that authority is what makes change happen, and forces you to build the thing that actually does.
